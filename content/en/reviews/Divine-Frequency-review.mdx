<Section icon={Swords} title="Combat and Progression Review">

Combat is built around more than raw damage. The available design supports firearms for reliable ranged pressure, psionic options for flexible utility or burst effects, and modifications that let players specialize their preferred approach. This structure gives encounters a tactical layer without removing the fast, direct feel expected from a retro shooter.

The best combat rhythm is deliberate rather than reckless. Players benefit from checking sightlines, preserving ammunition, identifying escape routes, and deciding when a powerful ability is more valuable than a standard attack. Early encounters also reward distance management. Closing in too quickly can turn a manageable enemy into a dangerous threat, while retreating through a known route often creates better opportunities.

| Combat Option | Primary Role | Best Use | Main Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Firearms | Consistent ranged damage | Open rooms, controlled target elimination, sustained encounters | Depends on ammunition and weapon handling |
| Psionic options | Utility, control, or burst pressure | Unusual encounters, groups, and situations requiring flexibility | Abilities may need careful timing and resource management |
| Modifications | Build specialization | Improving range, efficiency, handling, or effect strength | Upgrade materials may be limited during a first run |
| Hybrid loadout | Encounter coverage | Exploration-heavy areas with changing ranges and threats | Can become unfocused without a clear priority |

A major strength is the relationship between combat and resource management. Ammunition, healing, upgrades, and psionic resources should be treated as one survival budget. Spending everything on a minor encounter can make the next progression blocker much harder. This gives the game a more thoughtful pace than a shooter where every room can be cleared without consequence.

  <Step num="1" title="Choose One Reliable Damage Source">

<Section icon={Map} title="Exploration, Puzzles, and Horror Design">

Exploration is where Divine Frequency most clearly separates itself from a conventional shooter. The game uses branching routes, blocked access points, optional encounters, environmental clues, and hidden spaces to make navigation part of the horror. The player is often asked to remember a strange doorway, an unusual symbol, or a route that cannot yet be completed.

This design supports the game’s psychological tone. Uncertainty comes not only from enemies but also from incomplete information. A room may be dangerous, important, or simply suggestive, and the player must decide whether to investigate immediately or return later with better resources.

| Exploration Element | Player Response | Review Impact |
|---|---|---|
| Blocked routes | Mark them mentally and return after story progress | Encourages observation instead of repeated searching |
| Side rooms | Check them before major transitions | Adds resources, clues, and optional discoveries |
| Environmental symbols | Compare repeated and unique details | Supports puzzle solving without relying only on interface prompts |
| Unusual sound or lighting | Prepare before interacting | Builds tension around optional encounters |
| Hidden areas | Revisit earlier spaces after progression | Rewards players who maintain a route memory |

The puzzle approach is best handled progressively. First inspect the environment, then compare repeated details, and only afterward interact with the most suspicious object. This keeps discoveries from becoming random button hunting. The current material also supports spoiler-controlled exploration, which is useful for a game where atmosphere depends heavily on uncertainty.
